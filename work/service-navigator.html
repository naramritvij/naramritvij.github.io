Service Navigator

UX Research · Product Design · Figma · Moderated Usability Testing · Spring 2026
View live case study ↗ Solo UX Designer & Researcher

The problem

Newcomers to Canada often have to complete essential settlement tasks across separate government, financial and housing systems while learning unfamiliar terminology at the same time. For someone completing these processes for the first time, fragmentation and fear of making a mistake can make straightforward administrative tasks feel much harder than they need to be.

The product

Service Navigator is a guided web experience designed around five high-priority newcomer tasks: obtaining a Social Insurance Number, registering for OHIP, opening a bank account, searching for housing, and understanding first-time tax filing. Instead of sending users into a collection of disconnected resources, the experience turns each task into a structured workflow with preparation guidance, warnings, progress cues, and a clear explanation of what happens next.

My role

I worked as the solo UX designer and researcher, taking the project from problem framing through evaluation and refinement. Core design principles

  • Tunnel, don’t sprawl. Each screen should make the next action obvious and introduce choices only when a real decision is required.
  • Prepare before you proceed. Required documents and prerequisites appear before an application flow begins rather than halfway through it.
  • Warn early and consistently. Risk, scam and safety guidance uses a consistent visual language so users learn what to pay attention to.
  • Confirm and close. Every workflow ends by explaining what happens next, reducing uncertainty after task completion.

What the testing uncovered

The most important result was not the aggregate score; it was identifying where the interface could still mislead someone. A housing step tracker used checkmarks in a way that one participant interpreted as “already completed,” producing the only failed task in the test. Other sessions surfaced subtler issues: required tax items visually resembling optional content, unclear bank-filter multi-select behavior, an OHIP guidance note that looked optional, and inconsistent visual treatment across some flows.

Those findings became redesign priorities rather than being hidden behind the strong overall metrics. The exercise reinforced that a logically correct interface can still fail when the user interprets a visual cue differently from what the designer intended.

AI-assisted research workflow

AI tools supported parts of the research workflow rather than replacing user testing. Otter.ai and Fireflies were used for transcription, while Claude helped structure testing data, generate draft debrief questions, calculate aggregate metrics, and synthesize project documents. Domain-specific terms still required manual transcript correction, and human review was necessary to distinguish literal task completion from confident, low-friction completion.

What I learned

  • Reducing choice can be a service. For anxious first-time users, unnecessary optionality creates extra failure points.
  • Task completion is not the end of the experience. “What happens next?” guidance can determine whether a user leaves confident or uncertain.
  • Human testing reveals perceptual failures that automated review misses. The housing step-tracker issue appeared immediately in observation even though the flow was logically sound.
  • Design should assume the user has no informal support network. The strongest guidance works without requiring a friend, forum, or tutorial to fill in missing context.
